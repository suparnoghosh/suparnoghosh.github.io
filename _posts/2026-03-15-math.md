When I first started learning mathematics seriously, I assumed that the goal was always to *understand*.  
If something was confusing, that meant I had not yet studied it enough.

But over time I noticed something strange.

The moments when mathematics felt the most interesting were often the moments when I **did not understand what was going on**.

A theorem would appear whose statement seemed almost magical.  
A proof would use a technique that I had never seen before.  
A definition would suddenly make several earlier ideas fall into place — but only partially.

And for a while, everything would feel unclear.

At first this was frustrating.  
But eventually I realised that this state of confusion is not a bug of mathematics — it is **one of its most important features**.

Understanding in mathematics rarely happens all at once.  
Instead it seems to happen in layers.

You first learn the definitions.  
Then you learn the standard examples.  
Then you begin to see how the ideas interact.  
And much later, sometimes months or years afterwards, the picture becomes clearer.

Even then, the clarity is rarely complete.

For example, when I first encountered the **Euler–Lagrange equation**

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = 0,
$$

it felt like a miraculous formula appearing from nowhere.  
Only later did I begin to see how it arises naturally from the idea of varying a functional.

And even now, there are still aspects of the theory that feel mysterious.

But perhaps that is the point.

Mathematics seems to reward patience with confusion.  
You sit with a problem long enough, and slowly the pieces begin to align.

Not perfectly.  
But enough to keep going.

So this blog is going to be a place where I write about things that I **do not yet fully understand**:

- ideas that seem beautiful but mysterious  
- proofs that surprised me  
- concepts that took me a long time to grasp  
- and questions that I am still thinking about

In other words, this is a blog about the moments when I find myself saying:

> *I do not get it.*

And why that might be the best place to start learning.
